# ADR Causality Assessment

## 1. Introduction
Causality assessment evaluates whether an adverse drug reaction (ADR) is related to a suspected medicine. It is an essential part of pharmacovigilance.

## 2. Objectives
- Understand ADR causality assessment methods.
- Evaluate the time relationship between a drug and a reaction.
- Identify alternative causes.
- Classify reactions as Certain, Probable, or Possible.

## 3. WHO-UMC Causality Assessment

### Certain
The reaction has a plausible time relationship to the drug, cannot reasonably be explained by another cause, and is supported by convincing clinical evidence.

### Probable/Likely
The reaction has a reasonable time relationship to the drug, alternative causes are unlikely, and the response after stopping the drug is clinically appropriate.

### Possible
The reaction has a reasonable time relationship to the drug, but the disease or other medicines could also explain it.

## 4. Case Study 1: Certain

Patient: A patient develops anaphylaxis shortly after receiving penicillin. Clinical findings support an immediate allergic reaction, and no more likely alternative cause is identified.

- Suspected drug: Penicillin
- Reaction: Anaphylaxis
- Assessment: The timing and clinical presentation support a causal relationship.
- Classification: CERTAIN, assuming the complete clinical evidence meets the required criteria.

## 5. Case Study 2: Probable

A 55-year-old patient develops a persistent dry cough several weeks after starting enalapril for hypertension. Other common causes are considered unlikely. The cough resolves after the medicine is discontinued.

- Suspected drug: Enalapril
- Reaction: Persistent dry cough
- Assessment: The timing, known association, and improvement after withdrawal support a relationship.
- Classification: PROBABLE/LIKELY.

## 6. Case Study 3: Possible

A 45-year-old patient develops a skin rash and itching two days after starting amoxicillin for an infection. The underlying infection or another cause could also explain the symptoms. Further clinical information is unavailable.

- Suspected drug: Amoxicillin
- Reaction: Skin rash and itching
- Assessment: The timing supports a possible relationship, but alternative causes have not been excluded.
- Classification: POSSIBLE.

## 7. Naranjo Probability Scale

The Naranjo scale uses ten questions to estimate the probability that a drug caused an adverse reaction.

- Score 9 or above: Definite
- Score 5–8: Probable
- Score 1–4: Possible
- Score 0 or below: Doubtful

The Naranjo scale uses different category names from WHO-UMC, so the classifications are not directly interchangeable.

## 8. Conclusion

Causality assessment helps identify and evaluate suspected adverse drug reactions. WHO-UMC considers clinical timing, alternative causes, and response after drug withdrawal. The Naranjo scale provides a structured scoring method.

These methods support pharmacovigilance and safer medicine use.
